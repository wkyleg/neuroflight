# NeuroFlight Asset Research and Browser-Friendly Upgrade Plan

## Fast read

The highest-return path is **not** to replace NeuroFlight’s world with dozens of arbitrary GLB files. The codebase already defines two maps, `desert_expanse` and `ocean_islands`, with specific prop categories and counts; it also builds most repeated world props procedurally and renders many of them with `THREE.InstancedMesh`, which is exactly why the browser performance is still workable today. In practice, that means your best strategy is a **hybrid one**: keep repeated background clutter instanced or simplified; add a small number of prettier, stylized hero assets as landmarks; and swap in a single friendly plane model that actually fits the new direction. citeturn41view0turn21view0turn51view0turn42view0

## What the codebase says about asset strategy

NeuroFlight currently exposes two public map definitions. The desert map is configured around rocks, cacti, buildings, towers, mesas, dunes, ruins, and wrecks; the ocean map is configured around rocks, palm trees, pine trees, sailboats, cargo ships, whales, buoys, and lighthouses. The procedural asset type enum matches those categories exactly, which gives you a clean sourcing brief: you do **not** need generic “cool assets”; you need assets that map directly onto those named slots. citeturn41view0turn21view0

More importantly, the environment code is already optimized around **repeated procedural meshes**. `ProceduralWorld.ts` uses `THREE.InstancedMesh` for repeated trees, buildings, towers, dunes, ruins, wrecks, sailboats, cargo ships, whales, buoys, and lighthouses. That is a strong technical clue: if you replace all of those with arbitrary downloaded scenes that each bring their own material stack and draw calls, you will likely make the app look better in screenshots while making it worse in the browser. The right move is to preserve the current instancing logic for repeated clutter and reserve full GLB assets for **low-count landmarks** and **the player plane**. citeturn51view0

The sky and ring systems are also already procedural. `SkySystem.ts` uses the Three.js `Sky` addon and scene fog; `CloudSystem.ts` generates 30 billboard-style clouds on planes with a procedural canvas texture; `RingManager.ts` builds rings from torus geometry and adds its own glow mesh. So, the world does not actually need “ring assets,” and it does not urgently need a totally new sky system. It needs better **mid-distance silhouettes**, richer **ground composition**, and improved **materials, landmarks, and atmosphere tuning**. citeturn47view0turn46view0turn45view0

The asset loading layer is currently minimal. `AssetManager.ts` uses `GLTFLoader`, but there is no evidence in that file of Draco, KTX2, or Meshopt support. Three.js documents that `GLTFLoader` can be paired with `DRACOLoader`, `KTX2Loader`, and Meshopt decoding for compressed geometry and textures. In plain English: before you ingest a pile of new models, you should harden the loader path; otherwise you will be testing assets against a weaker-than-necessary runtime. citeturn42view0turn33search0turn35search1turn35search4

## Tripo prompt set for custom asset generation

Tripo’s own material says it can generate models from text or images and export in GLB, FBX, OBJ, USD, STL, and 3MF; for your browser pipeline, **GLB** is the first choice because glTF is the runtime delivery format Three.js and modern real-time pipelines are built around. Use Tripo when you cannot find the right stylized ground filler, or when you need several assets that share a unified tone. citeturn37search1turn37search2turn37search9turn36search20

The most productive prompt structure for NeuroFlight is this:

```text
stylized low-poly [asset];
friendly kid-safe art direction;
rounded silhouettes;
single shared material or very few materials;
flat or softly hand-painted shading;
game-ready;
clean topology;
under [triangle budget];
separate simple pivots;
no tiny greebles;
no baked lighting;
exportable as GLB;
readable from far above in a browser flight game
```

## Three.js upgrades that will multiply the value of better assets

The biggest technical multiplier is to **upgrade the GLTF ingestion path**. Three.js documents `GLTFLoader` support for Draco-compressed geometry, KTX2 compressed textures, and Meshopt-compressed buffers; NeuroFlight’s `AssetManager` currently uses a bare `GLTFLoader`. Add those loaders before you do large-scale asset testing, because the difference between “browser chokes on imported art” and “browser handles it fine” is often just geometry and texture compression. citeturn42view0turn33search0turn35search1turn35search4

The second multiplier is to make your import pipeline **glTF-native**. The Khronos glTF spec describes glTF as a runtime asset delivery format; `gltfpack` is explicitly built to reduce download size and improve loading and rendering speed; and glTF Transform gives you reproducible CLI inspection and optimization tools. For NeuroFlight, that means a sane asset workflow is: download or generate asset; convert to GLB if needed; inspect; optimize; compress; then test. citeturn36search20turn36search4turn36search0turn36search1turn36search3

The third multiplier is to respect the current **instancing-first** world design. Use prettier downloaded assets in one of two ways only: either as **rare landmarks** through the map’s optional `landmarks` mechanism, or as re-authored low-material meshes that can still fit an instancing strategy for repeated clutter. The maps and type system already support landmarks, while the environment generator already shows that instanced repeated props are the baseline performance model. That is why Quaternius and Kenney matter so much; their packs are already biased toward low-poly shared-material workflows. citeturn21view0turn51view0turn53search6turn25search0

The fourth multiplier is **environment rendering polish**, but applied with restraint. Three.js already provides the `Sky` addon, `Fog` and `FogExp2`, `Water`, `EffectComposer`, and `UnrealBloomPass`; your repo already uses the `Sky` addon and fog, and the README says the game uses bloom post-processing. The productive move here is not “more effects everywhere”; it is a modest pass on atmospheric depth, water appearance on the ocean map, and selective bloom or glow for rings, HUD targets, and reward moments. Tightly controlled atmospheric perspective will do more for perceived richness than another dozen random props. citeturn47view0turn16view0turn33search2turn34search1turn34search4turn35search2turn34search3turn35search3

My concrete implementation order would be this: first, fix the aircraft asset mismatch; second, add compression support and an optimization step; third, create **one desert landmark set** and **one ocean landmark set** using stylized assets; fourth, replace the current plane with one friendly canonical aircraft; fifth, add a handful of mid-air altitude cues such as hot air balloons and better cloud clusters; sixth, tune sky, fog, and water; and only then start replacing repeated background clutter. That order is conservative, but it is the order that is least likely to destroy performance while still producing visible change quickly. citeturn40view0turn38view0turn42view0turn33search0turn35search1turn35search4turn51view0turn47view0turn46view0

## Open questions and limitations

I was able to inspect the public GitHub repo and the public asset sources, but I did **not** do a full runtime audit of every rendering path end-to-end; the repository was browsable, yet not indexed in the way that would make a deeper codebase walkthrough efficient. So the conclusions here are strongest on **asset strategy, source quality, licensing, and web-performance fit**, and slightly weaker on exact scene integration details deeper in the runtime. citeturn16view0turn50view0

A few licensing caveats matter. Quaternius, Kenney, Poly Haven, and ambientCG are the safest bulk sources because they are explicitly permissive or CC0. Poly Pizza is highly useful, but its results mix licenses; use its API or filters to separate CC0 from CC BY. Sketchfab is excellent for single models, but its free downloads may be Creative Commons or Standard License, and Creative Commons cases require attribution. ShareTextures is excellent for internal use, but its custom CC0-based terms still restrict redistribution of the raw assets themselves. citeturn53search5turn25search0turn13search9turn31search11turn28search11turn52search0turn52search2turn32search1

The last open design question is artistic rather than technical: whether NeuroFlight’s first visual pivot should be **storybook aviation** or **abstract toy-flight**. Given your stated goals, I would choose storybook aviation for now; one friendly biplane, lush stylized terrain, bright maritime props, and playful landmarks. It is a smaller leap from the current code and mechanics, and it will make the eventual transition toward a kid-facing focus and self-regulation experience feel deliberate rather than chaotic.
